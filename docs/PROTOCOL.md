# BioRegional Commons

**A practical operating system for people organizing as members of living ecosystems.**

BioRegional Commons is an open, place-based framework for communities that want to organize around the real ecological and social systems they belong to: watersheds, ecoregions, neighborhoods, food systems, care networks, local economies, and shared culture.

It is designed for:

- Ecoregional chapters
- Watershed circles
- Community learning hubs
- Creator commons
- Field stations
- Maker and repair labs
- Mutual-aid networks
- Community solution labs
- Ecovillages, farms, schools, nonprofits, and arts collectives

> **The operating sentence:** People listen to one another and to the land; organize at the scale of watersheds and ecosystems; make transparent, reversible decisions; build useful projects; teach what they learn; circulate value locally; measure real effects; and adapt together.

## What This Project Is

BioRegional Commons translates the Green Fire Creator Commons framework into a complete protocol for ecosystem-centered community organization.

The system brings together:

- Community listening and ecological observation
- Watershed- and bioregion-scale mapping
- Nested, transparent governance
- Community care and mutual support
- Project design, prototyping, and maintenance
- Place-based education and youth participation
- Local exchange, jobs, and benefit sharing
- Media, storytelling, data stewardship, and AI governance
- Monitoring, learning, and adaptive management
- Federation between local chapters without erasing local identity

This is not a claim of territorial authority. A chapter is a voluntary commons and coordinating body. It does not automatically represent an entire watershed, bioregion, Indigenous nation, neighborhood, or species community. Its legitimacy comes from consent, open participation, transparent scope, accountable relationships, and useful work.

## The Double Mandate

Every decision must answer two questions:

1. **What does the community need?**
2. **What does this living place require or permit?**

A project that answers only one of these questions is incomplete.

## Core Commitments

Every BioRegional Commons chapter is expected to uphold the following principles:

- **Dual intake:** Human needs are heard through community listening, while ecological conditions are understood through observation, mapping, data, and seasonal indicators.
- **Nested governance:** Decisions are made at the smallest scale capable of responsibly holding the consequences.
- **Rights before efficiency:** Land access, privacy, cultural knowledge, Indigenous rights, youth safety, and affected-party consent cannot be overridden by a simple majority vote.
- **Projects before branding:** Trust is earned through visible usefulness, not claims or image alone.
- **Care, culture, ecology, and economy belong together:** They are treated as one operating system, not separate programs.
- **Monitoring must change decisions:** Every major project includes a baseline, target, review date, and adaptation rule.
- **AI assists but does not govern:** AI may help organize information, but it cannot decide legitimacy, rights, funding worthiness, truth, punishment, or who deserves care.
- **Knowledge should travel responsibly:** Methods are documented so other places can adapt them without copying or extracting local culture.

## The 12-Stage Living Loop

BioRegional Commons work moves through a repeatable cycle:

| Stage | Core question | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Locate** | Where are we ecologically and culturally? | Working boundary and map stack |
| **2. Listen** | What are people experiencing, needing, and imagining? | Community intake and affected-party map |
| **3. Observe** | What are water, soil, species, climate, food, energy, and materials showing? | Bioregional baseline |
| **4. Map** | Where are stress, care, assets, flows, and opportunities? | Living Commons Atlas |
| **5. Convene** | Who must be present for a legitimate next decision? | Council and rights-holder process |
| **6. Prioritize** | What is urgent, regenerative, feasible, and maintainable? | Seasonal priority list |
| **7. Design** | What is the smallest useful and safe experiment? | Project brief and safeguards |
| **8. Build** | Who does what, with which tools, money, and proof? | Prototype or service |
| **9. Teach & Tell** | How does the work become shared knowledge and culture? | Workshop, story, guide, or media |
| **10. Exchange & Care** | How does value return to contributors, affected people, and place? | Pay, credits, access, and mutual support |
| **11. Measure** | What changed, for whom, and with what uncertainty? | Impact and learning report |
| **12. Adapt & Replicate** | What should stop, continue, change, or travel? | Next cycle and reusable toolkit |

## The Seven Engines

The operating system is organized through seven connected engines:

| Engine | Mandate | Core output |
|---|---|---|
| **Council** | Make legitimate, transparent, repairable decisions | Decision log, budget, mandates, review dates |
| **Bioregional** | Observe land, water, climate, species, food, energy, and materials | Seasonal dashboard and priority map |
| **Media** | Make local work visible, teachable, fundable, and accountable | Consent-based stories and public reports |
| **Maker** | Repair, prototype, fabricate, test, and maintain solutions | Working assets and build guides |
| **Exchange** | Circulate money, credits, tools, care, and jobs locally | Contribution ledger and benefit flow |
| **Data / AI** | Organize knowledge without extracting or replacing people | Protected data commons and transparent AI log |
| **Culture** | Create belonging, memory, celebration, ritual, and public participation | Gatherings connected to real projects |

## Organizing at the Right Scale

BioRegional Commons uses four nested scales:

| Scale | Primary work | Decision body |
|---|---|---|
| **Site / neighborhood** | Daily care, projects, learning, tools, food, mutual aid | Site circle |
| **Subwatershed / watershed** | Water, flooding, habitat, monitoring, cross-neighborhood work | Watershed circle |
| **Bioregion** | Shared priorities, economy, education, media, and regional infrastructure | Bioregional council and assembly |
| **Ecoregional learning network** | Peer learning, ecological comparison, seed and tool exchange | Federated network |

The rule of scale is simple:

> Organize daily work locally, water work by watershed, shared infrastructure and culture bioregionally, and ecological learning across the ecoregion.

## The Living Commons Atlas

The map is not decoration. It is a shared memory and decision-making tool.

A complete atlas may include:

1. Ecoregions
2. Watersheds and flow direction
3. Water systems
4. Land and soil
5. Habitat and biodiversity
6. Climate stress and hazards
7. Human settlement and accessibility
8. Care and essential systems
9. Skills, spaces, tools, and institutions
10. Food, energy, material, labor, money, and information flows
11. Culture, history, and community memory
12. Active projects, maintenance, and outcomes

Sensitive species locations, sacred places, cultural knowledge, private wells, personal information, and vulnerable residents must never be published without appropriate permission.

## Governance

The governance model combines place-based representation with functional stewardship.

Possible governing bodies include:

- Site circles
- Functional circles
- Watershed circles
- A bioregional council
- An open community assembly
- A legal stewardship board or fiduciary body

### Decision methods

Different decisions require different methods:

- **Information:** Publish or notify.
- **Reversible operations:** Delegated decision after advice.
- **Projects, policies, and budgets:** Consent within an approved mandate.
- **Participatory funds:** Community ranking plus feasibility, safety, ecological, and rights review.
- **Major or irreversible decisions:** Supermajority or consensus, affected-party process, written analysis, and reconsideration period.
- **Personal data, private land access, cultural knowledge, or Indigenous knowledge:** Explicit permission from the holder.
- **Emergency actions:** Temporary action by an incident lead within clearly written limits, followed by rapid review.

Every council agenda includes a **Land Seat**: a steward who presents ecological observations, seasonal conditions, downstream effects, uncertainty, and red flags. The Land Seat does not claim to literally speak for nature; it ensures that more-than-human consequences are not omitted.

## The Green Fire Quest

Projects move through a structured pathway:

1. Signal
2. Listening
3. Baseline
4. Council review
5. Research
6. Co-design
7. Resource plan
8. Prototype
9. Teach and tell
10. Test
11. Decide
12. Report and replicate

Every project should define:

- The need and place
- The desired measurable condition
- Rights, consent, access, and cultural protocols
- Ecological fit
- The smallest useful experiment
- Roles, labor, and decision rights
- Budget, tools, materials, permits, and insurance
- Proof, indicators, and uncertainty
- Stop and adaptation triggers
- Maintenance and end-of-life plans
- Learning, media, and benefit sharing

A high project score never overrides a red flag, missing consent, unsafe conditions, ecological harm, or the absence of a maintenance owner.

## Physical and Distributed Hubs

A commons does not need to begin with a large campus. A minimum viable network can start with:

- One borrowed meeting room
- One field-learning relationship
- One community-care relationship
- A mobile tool or media kit
- A reliable public and protected record system
- One useful, maintainable project

Possible hub types include:

- Council and Culture House
- Field Station
- Maker and Repair Lab
- Learning Commons
- Media and Data Lab
- Food, Seed, and Water Node
- Resilience and Care Node

## Care as Infrastructure

BioRegional Commons recognizes that ecological work fails when people are exhausted, excluded, unpaid, unsafe, or unsupported.

Care infrastructure may include:

- Meals and food support
- Transportation
- Accessibility accommodations
- Child and elder care
- Emergency communication
- Conflict support
- Rest, rotation, and backup roles
- Fair compensation
- Private needs intake
- Mutual-aid matching

Care work, creative work, governance, ecological work, technical work, and learning are all recognized as real contributions.

## Local Exchange and Benefit Sharing

The system can support:

- Paid staff and contractors
- Apprenticeships
- Community credits
- Volunteers
- Work trade
- Revenue sharing and co-creation

Credits should supplement fair wages, not disguise unpaid labor. Speculative tokens are not part of the minimum system.

Before work begins, contributors should understand:

- The type of work relationship
- Compensation or credit value
- Ownership and intellectual property
- Verification requirements
- Cancellation and exit terms
- Revenue distribution
- Community and ecological return

## Media, Data, and AI

Stories and data should serve the people and places they come from.

Media work should address:

- Purpose
- Consent
- Context
- Dignity and safety
- Sensitive locations
- Review before publication
- Withdrawal options
- Benefit sharing
- Long-term control of archives

### AI may assist with

- Meeting summaries
- Scheduling
- Translation
- Drafting
- Map cleanup
- Skill matching
- Grant research
- Indicator reports
- Searchable archives

### AI may not decide

- Membership worth
- Punishment
- Project legitimacy
- Funding winners
- Cultural permission
- Land rights
- Safety clearance
- The truth of contested testimony
- Who deserves care

Whenever AI materially shapes a public report, map, plan, match, or recommendation, the chapter should log the tool, purpose, data class, human reviewer, known limits, and correction path.

## Monitoring and Adaptive Management

Every major project should include:

1. **Baseline:** What was true before the intervention?
2. **Desired condition:** What should change, by when, and for whom?
3. **Method:** Who measures, how often, and with what quality standard?
4. **Decision trigger:** What result causes continuation, adaptation, pause, escalation, or stopping?
5. **Stewardship horizon:** Who checks again after the initial project period?

A quarterly learning review asks:

- What changed?
- What did not change?
- What unintended effects appeared?
- Whose experience is missing?
- What should stop?
- What should adapt?
- What should continue or expand?
- What knowledge can travel?

## Start a Chapter

### First 30 days: Locate and listen

- Form a founding team with distinct responsibilities
- Declare a provisional scope
- Identify Indigenous nations, watershed groups, community organizations, and existing institutions
- Hold listening sessions
- Start the map stack
- Inventory skills, spaces, tools, and relationships
- Publish a clear statement of what the chapter does and does not represent

### By 90 days: Build the minimum viable chapter

- Create a baseline
- Adopt a working charter
- Define roles, decision rights, privacy, conflict, youth, cultural, and data protocols
- Establish a hub or host agreement
- Choose and complete one visible, low-risk, maintainable project
- Publish a public report and money summary
- Name and prepare backups for critical roles

### By 12 months: Become an accountable local institution

- Maintain a monthly council rhythm
- Complete and monitor multiple projects
- Establish youth learning and care systems
- Run a participatory budget
- Publish a State of the Bioregion report
- Review finances, access, conflicts, roles, and indicators
- Release at least one reusable project toolkit

## Minimum Viable Chapter Test

A chapter should be able to answer **yes** to the following:

- Can anyone see what area and people the chapter does and does not represent?
- Can a person submit a need, receive a response, and appeal a decision?
- Can the council name its watershed, ecoregion, seasonal risks, and baseline indicators?
- Are decision rights, conflicts of interest, budgets, and review dates documented?
- Are cultural knowledge, youth, personal data, rights-holders, and sensitive ecological information protected?
- Has one useful project been completed and maintained?
- Can contributors distinguish paid, credit, work-trade, apprentice, and volunteer work?
- Does AI remain advisory and logged?
- Can the group continue functioning if one founder steps away?
- Has the group publicly reported what failed, changed, or was stopped?

## Safety and Legal Limits

This framework is an organizational manual. It does not replace:

- Legal counsel
- Environmental engineering
- Ecological assessment
- Land-title review
- Permits and zoning review
- Insurance
- Youth safeguarding
- Public-health protocols
- Emergency management
- Government-to-government or rights-holder consultation

Qualified professionals and appropriate authorities should be involved whenever consequences exceed the group’s competence.

## Final Operating Sentence

> Community needs are heard. Ecological conditions are observed. Rights and relationships are honored. Decisions are made at the appropriate scale. Youth design. Elders and knowledge holders guide with consent. Makers build. Artists tell the story. Care sustains the people. Exchange circulates value. Data is protected. AI assists but never rules. Outcomes are measured. The group adapts. What works becomes a pattern another place can responsibly learn from.
